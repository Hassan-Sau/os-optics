# How the local viewer draws materials

`os-optics view` draws a run in two ways: PNG snapshots, by a NumPy rasteriser that needs no browser, and a browser page, by
WebGL. Both use one material model, so the image in a report and what you see in the page are the same glass. The numbers live
in one place, `src/os_optics/viewer/shading.py`; the page receives them in its view document (`shading`) and its fragment shader
evaluates the same equations.

## What a mesh's material comes from

The run's `optical_scene.glb` states the finish of each material. Everything reading the view, the viewer included, reads it
from there:

| In the GLB material | Meaning | Source |
|---|---|---|
| `baseColorFactor` | the material's identity colour and alpha (the legend's swatches) | `STATIC_MATERIALS`, `glb.py` |
| `metallicFactor`, `roughnessFactor` | the finish | `MATERIAL_FINISHES`, `glb.py` (table below) |
| `KHR_materials_transmission` | light passes: it is glass | the finish |
| `KHR_materials_ior` | the refractive index of the glass the surface bounds, at the d line (587.56 nm), or at the middle of a material's band that does not reach it | the run's own material (`display_media` of `optical_scene.json`) |
| `KHR_materials_volume.thicknessFactor` | the glass's edge-to-edge extent along the element's local z, in mm | the drawn surfaces |

Where the model does not state a glass (an unmodelled substrate) the index extension is absent and glTF's default of 1.5 applies.
One material name has one glTF material per glass and thickness, so names keep identifying what is drawn.

## The model

Glass and metal (`transmission > 0` or `metallic > 0`) are lit by a studio that turns with the camera. Directions are in the camera
frame: x to the right of the screen, y up the screen, z towards the viewer. With `n` the surface normal on the viewer's side,
`v` the direction to the viewer and `c = n . v`:

- **Environment** `E(d, r)`: a dark floor and a bright sky meeting at a soft horizon, dimmed behind the scene, plus broad lights (`LIGHTS`), each a
  smooth lobe `exp((d . c - 1) / s)` with no edge. A surface's roughness `r` widens a lobe and lowers its peak. Whatever a dome faces, its upper part
  reflects sky and its lower part floor, and a lobe's reflection rolls over it as a soft highlight. A surface seen edge-on reflects what lies behind the scene: a dark mirror.
- **Fresnel**: `F = F0 + (1 - F0) (1 - c)^5` with `F0 = ((ior - 1) / (ior + 1))^2`; a metal's `F0` is its colour. A surface seen at a
  grazing angle therefore reflects far more than one seen square-on.
- **Reflection**: `S = F (E(2 c n - v, r) + H) (1 - GROUND_DIM r)`, `H` being the GGX highlight of one key light; a ground edge stays quiet.
- **Glass**: the ray refracted by Snell's law crosses the path `p = t / sqrt(1 - (1 - c^2) / ior^2)`, `t` being the thickness. Absorption is
  `A = 1 - exp(-sigma p)`, with `sigma` set from the material's alpha (`GLASS_ABSORPTION`, `REFERENCE_THICKNESS_MM`). What lies
  behind passes as `T = (1 - mean(F)) (1 - A)`. The absorbed part shows the glass's colour `b`: `src = D(S) + A D(b L)`, `L` being a soft light on the
  surface and `D` the display encoding below, and the coverage is `a = 1 - T (1 - max(src))`: what it shows also covers.
- **Coated and modelled faces** (filter, splitter, polarizer, waveplate, thin lens, attenuator, Faraday rotator) absorb a fixed share of their alpha whatever
  their thickness, so they keep the colour the legend shows; they are lit as glass otherwise.
- **Satin and metal** (not glass): `src = D(S + (1 - metallic) b L) alpha`, `a = alpha`.
- **Output**: `D(x)` tone maps (`1 - exp(-EXPOSURE x)`) and encodes to sRGB; `src` is blended premultiplied, `out = src + dst (1 - a)`, far to near, so a
  stack of glass saturates softly instead of adding up to white. The snapshot layers each pixel; the page sorts by object.

A curved surface sweeps its normal across the studio, so a light appears on it as a highlight whose shape follows the curvature, and
the reflectance climbs towards its edge. A flat surface shows one tone. This is what lets a lens read as curved glass without labels.

Between two glasses (a cemented interface) the index in the GLB is their ratio and the thickness 0, on the whole surface, its rim too. It reflects a few thousandths:
a faint curve inside the glass, no band. The rim and the edge wall are ground glass, see-through. The band that marks the clear aperture is a faint part of the glass;
**Clear aperture** in the page marks it, and the tracer's clear aperture is always in the section (`--section`) and the surface's facts.

Outlines: the zones a surface is drawn in are welded first, so their seams are not lines. What is drawn is the surface's real edge and, for each view, its silhouette, where it
turns away from the eye, which gives a dome its curved outline against the dark backdrop. The rasteriser and the page draw the same edges (`src/os_optics/viewer/outline.py`).
The page's perspective is a long lens (a 14 degree field of view), so a surface seen from the side stays side-on across the picture, also close up.

The renderer does not refract: what lies behind glass is not displaced, and beams are drawn where the Engine traced them.

The backdrop is a dark gradient in the Optical Simulation View, where glass shows by what it reflects, and stays light in the
Experimental Lab Assembly, whose parts show by their class colours. A beam colour with less than 3 : 1 contrast against the dark backdrop
(a blue or a violet) gets a light casing under its line; its colour and the legend stay as they are.

## Views

`iso` shows a straight optical layout from the near side: 14 degrees off square-on and 12 degrees up, which is about 76 degrees off the axis, with
the path level on the screen. Seen within about 40 degrees of its axis any lens is a disc. A folded layout keeps a 3/4 view from above.

`path_horizontal` looks square-on at the main beam, orthographic, which then runs exactly from left to right whatever its direction in the
design. `plane_xy`, `plane_zx` and `plane_yz` look square-on at a coordinate plane, orthographic, with the plane's first letter to the right and its
second up the screen. They are views of the whole system, not cuts: `--section` draws cuts. All of these are available to `--views` too.

In the page, **Roll -15°** and **Roll +15°** turn the picture about the view direction; dragging then rotates about the new screen up. With **Turn about beam**
on, a drag across the beam turns the view about the beam, like rolling a rod under your hand, and a drag along it does nothing, for a beam along any axis.

## Checks

`tests/test_viewer_materials.py` checks the equations against their closed forms, the index and thickness in the GLB against the prescription's own numbers,
and the presets for beams along many axes. `tests/test_viewer.py` checks the packaged, self-contained viewer page and local server;
`tests/test_viewer_browser_pose.py` exercises its real browser camera against the independent Python snapshot pose across
window sizes and pixel ratios. `scripts/viewer-bundle/package-lock.json` pins the standalone page's vendored three.js build input.
